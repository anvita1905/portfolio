Beyond the Headlines: Untangling the Competing Accounts of India’s Youth Protests

“Democracy is based upon the conviction that there are extraordinary possibilities in ordinary people.”
— Harry Emerson Fosdick

On 6 June 2026, thousands of young Indians gathered at Jantar Mantar in New Delhi for the first in-person demonstration organized by the Cockroach Janta Party (CJP). While protesters described the march as a peaceful movement against examination irregularities and systemic failures in education, authorities largely characterized it as a politically motivated agitation requiring heightened security. The contrasting accounts reveal that the purpose of the demonstrations depends largely on whose narrative is being told. The protests were brought about due to deep frustration over youth unemployment, shrinking economic prospects, tragic student suicides linked to exam stress, and demanded the resignation of the former Education Minister of India, Dharmendra Pradhan.

Sonam Wangchuk, a renowned activist, went on a hunger strike to advocate for the CJP movement. A few days later, the 59-year-old was taken to the hospital due to his critical condition, prompting indignation among the protestors.

Monday’s protest was among the largest witnessed by the nation in recent years, with the nature and breadth of its grievances alongside the scale of the demonstrations making it unlike many of the protests against the Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) in the past decade. CJP’s reach and digital momentum also reinforced the anti-corruption movement of 2011.

Some believe that the CJP movement is just a catalyst for a recurring issue of the state’s disregard towards the youth and the common man who rely on subsidies and opportunities granted by the government in pursuit of social and financial mobility. Prime Minister Narendra Modi addressed the issue via social media for the first time, acknowledging the demonstrators’ anger and announcing the formation of fast-track courts. However, it is uncertain whether the PM’s assurances are sufficient to defuse the movement.

The police lathi-charged and used tear gas for dispersion against the CJP’s march to the Parliament in Delhi on Monday, injuring several people. The people suffered fractures, swellings, and head injuries.

The grievances articulated by CJP resonated beyond a single localized constituency, and the movement eventually spread to 34 cities across India, drawing thousands of protestors nationwide.

Following the resignation and replacement of former Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan, CJP withdrew its protests.

The Cockroach Janta Party’s vision is to bring Gen Z into the political sphere by providing them with political representation. Their manifesto lists their 5 primary demands regarding parliamentary oversight.

Apart from the exam paper leaks, several peripheral factors listed in their charter added to their indignation. These include enacting a new public examinations act, establishing the NTA, SSC, and National Examination bodies, adopting a student rights charter, and creating a national aspirant welfare fund.

CJP publicly reiterated three central demands:

  • Dharmendra Pradhan’s resignation;
  • ₹1 crore compensation for families of students who died by suicide;
  • no prosecution of peaceful protesters.

The Indian Express reported CJP as saying that the protest would continue until these demands were met.

CJP transitioned from demanding accountability for the examination system to demanding accountability for the state’s response to the protests.

Following the resignation of former Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan, CJP updated its “demand roster,” asking for a public apology from the RAF and Delhi Police, which remains pending.

Delhi Police framed the protests primarily as an unauthorized gathering that posed a law-and-order concern. After criticism over the extension of National Security Act detention powers, Delhi Police explicitly said that the renewal was not connected to the CJP protests. It characterized the order as a routine quarterly renewal that had been issued before the demonstrations began. During the confrontation, Delhi Police said the protest was being handled professionally and emphasized that the march lacked authorization. Reuters also reported that Delhi Police appealed to protesters to maintain peace and cooperate with officers to maintain public order.

Health Minister J. P. Nadda met CJP representatives and said the government would consider their demands. The Delhi government approved the withdrawal of 13 FIRs connected with the CJP protests. Its Home Department said that no further adverse legal action would be taken against people involved in the protests, although that protection did not extend to people with criminal antecedents. State authorities largely framed the immediate issue in terms of legality and public order. At the same time, the government’s subsequent engagement and eventual withdrawal of cases against protesters demonstrated a parallel willingness to negotiate with the movement.

However, official statements and demands constitute only two sides of the narrative; for the youth, the picture was less black-and-white. I interviewed Aavika, 18, a Mumbai resident and a student who appeared for NEET with the intention of pursuing veterinary medicine. For her, the examination was “the main pathway” toward a career she had invested herself in. After the allegations of irregularities surfaced, the subsequent retest forced her to repeat months of preparation while dealing with additional academic, emotional, and financial pressure. The uncertainty also complicated her counselling and admission plans. Aavika said the authorities needed clearer and more consistent communication. She felt that the crisis was fundamentally an educational issue rather than an administrative problem. Her trust in the examination system weakened, although she relied more heavily on official statements and mainstream reporting when information was corroborated across multiple credible sources.

I also interviewed 15 other participants between the ages of 17 and 46. The responses collected showed that social media largely influenced respondents’ initial understanding of the protests.

Respondents pointed out that some aspects received considerably less attention, including people looking after the logistics of the demonstrations; distributing food and water; and cleaning the streets after the protests. The responses exhibited uncertainty over the protesters’ motives and the reliability of the intel circulating online.

They perceived mainstream media coverage as presenting an ostensibly neutral account while appearing implicitly opposed to the demonstrations. Another recurring difficulty lay in distinguishing between competing accounts of events. Of the 15 people I interviewed, 4 encountered claims on social media about deaths during the protests that they later understood to have been officially disputed.

While the interview provides a firsthand account of the examination crisis, it does not establish what happened on the ground during the protests, since most of my respondents followed the demonstrations remotely. They represent how differently the same demonstrations could appear depending on the source through which they were encountered.

The divergence between the CJP and the authorities lay in how the grievances were characterized. The CJP advocated for accountability and representation, while the authorities were concerned about the authorization and the legality of the demonstrations. Nevertheless, the scale of the protests resists a single interpretation. These demonstrations were expressions of broader concerns about the ability of young people to influence public decision-making.

However, important questions remain about whether authorities will respond to these grievances through reforms, and whether protest movements can render public anger into sustained political participation. Such events require distinguishing documented facts from official and public perceptions. The way institutions respond to dissent will determine the magnitude and extent of public trust and the legitimacy of future forms of political expression in India.
